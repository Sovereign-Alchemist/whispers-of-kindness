Our Story

This began with a question. Someone asked an AI what human beings would be remembered for, and kindness did not make the list.

The answer stayed with us. Not because the machine was necessarily wrong, but because it was reading the record we actually left behind. It had been handed our written history and asked to sum us up, and it summed us up honestly. Kindness simply was not in the file.

That is not a complaint about machines, or about historians, or about anyone reading the record carefully. It is a fact about paperwork.

What gets written down

We write down our victories and our losses. We preserve the stories of wars and atrocities, discoveries and acquisitions, moments that changed the course of history. Cruelty in particular leaves a heavy trail, because cruelty tends to be performed for an audience, or investigated, or punished, and every one of those things generates a document.

Kindness leaves almost nothing. It is the most practised and least recorded thing people do. Nobody writes, "I was generous today," in a diary. You make dinner for someone who is hungry. You bring a meal to a neighbour. You stay a little longer. You sit with someone through a difficult time. You care for someone when they need you. Then life continues, and the kindness becomes part of your day rather than part of your record.

That wordlessness is part of what makes it genuine. It is also why the record leans the way it does. Not because kindness is absent from human life, but because so much of it never went into words at all. It went into the meal, the mended coat, the garden somebody else got to eat from.

So anyone reading the written record for evidence of human character, a historian, a grandchild, a machine, would reasonably conclude that people are worse than they are. The reading would be careful. The file is just incomplete.

The answer to that is not to argue with the file. It is to put something in it, on purpose.

Why recipes

Kindness did leave one written trace, almost by accident, and it is sitting in kitchen drawers.

Feeding people had to be done again and again, and it had to be remembered exactly, so it got written down when almost nothing else about caring for someone did. A recipe card is a set of instructions for repeating an act of care. That is the whole reason it exists on paper.

So recipes are the medium here, not the point. Food is simply what kindness chose, because food was the thing that needed the note. The same care is there in a hand-knit sweater, in a quilt pieced together over a long winter, in a driveway shoveled before anyone else is awake. None of those left a document behind them. The potato salad did.

Many of those cards are still out there. In drawers and boxes and the backs of cupboards, disappearing one household at a time as the people who kept them go.

What we are actually gathering

The recipe is where the story begins. It is not the whole of it.

What you know about the person who made it, who they made it for, or what it meant in your family tells us what the card itself never could. Sometimes you may know the whole story. Sometimes you may know only a name, a place, a small detail, or the memory of someone standing at a stove.

Not every recipe will come with a story. Not every story will be known to us. Every recipe is still worth giving a place to.

Recipes are chosen for the strength of the story attached to them, rather than for culinary merit. An ordinary recipe with a rich history is exactly what this archive exists for. A flawless recipe with nothing behind it is not.

Every submission is read by hand. That is slow, and it is meant to be.

from the archive

One of the first recipes sent to us is a Christmas cake, and the story attached to it begins at a bus stop.

In 1953 Lois Sagle moved to Ottawa so her husband could finish his medical internship. She got talking to another woman while they were waiting for a bus, and it turned into a friendship that lasted the rest of her life. The family came to know her as Red.

The two of them decided to make Christmas cake together that first year. Nobody now remembers where the recipe came from. They more than doubled it, because they were baking for two households, and then they ran out of anything big enough to mix it in, so the whole batch went into the bathtub. A very clean bathtub, her daughter is careful to point out.

Her daughter grew up hearing that story every December, and this year she sent it to us with the recipe.

Lois Sagle's Christmas cake, submitted by her daughter Ana-La-Rai Sagle, Vancouver Island.

What arrives, and why it takes that shape

Once a month, one recipe is chosen, collaged by hand, and mailed out as a keepsake card.

The front carries the recipe as it was originally written. The back carries the archive record: who made it, who they made it for, where and when, and how it travelled to reach you. Where possible, a reproduction of the original handwriting itself.

That back face is the reason the whole thing exists. A recipe with no person attached is a set of instructions. A recipe with the person attached is a record of somebody caring for somebody else, on a date, in a place, by name. One of those belongs in a cookbook. The other belongs in an archive.

It arrives as an object because an object survives differently than a file does. Paper gets kept in a drawer by people who never meant to archive anything, which is exactly how these recipes reached us in the first place.

This matters more now than it did ten years ago. As machines make endless words, the things only a lived life can make become precious. A machine can write forever. It cannot make the potato salad your aunt brought every summer, or hold the reason she kept bringing it.

As the archive grows

There is no back catalogue here. This is the beginning of one.

What is being built is slow on purpose. One recipe a month, gathered, read, and mailed, is not many recipes in a year. It is a great many in a decade, and the decade is the point of an archive.

So the honest answer about what comes next is: more of this, and more room to hold it. As recipes arrive, more of them get a place, and the record of the people behind them gets thicker and harder to lose. The work does not change shape as it grows. It accumulates.

We would rather promise that and keep it than promise something larger and stay vague about when.

If you have one of these cards

Perhaps there is one tucked away somewhere, stained from years of use, written in a familiar hand. Perhaps you remember who made it, or who sat around the table. Whatever you know, we would love to hear it.

Send us the recipe, and whatever you remember about the person who made it. That is how this grows: one card, one story, one act of kindness at a time.

The recipe was never really about the food. Kindness was the meal.
